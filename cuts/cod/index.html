The Cut Guide

Cod

Also sold as: atlantic cod · pacific cod · cod loin · scrod · salt cod · bacalao · bacalhau

Cod is about 81 percent water and under 1 percent fat — it stores its fat in its liver instead of its muscle, which is where cod liver oil comes from. Those big flakes are wide muscle blocks held by thin connective seams, and both the seams and the water go at almost the same temperature. Cook it to 130°F and it's dense and custardy; take it to 145°F and it turns into wet cotton that won't stay on the spatula.

This is a cut guide, not a recipe — what this cut is, why it behaves the way it does, and which of our 7 dishes use it. Recipes stay ad-free; guides like this one carry a single ad.

What it is

Cod is a groundfish that spends its life cruising near the bottom rather than sprinting, and it does most of that work with white muscle organized into unusually large myotomes — big blocks, widely spaced connective sheets — which is why cod flakes come off in slabs instead of shreds. It stores energy in a large liver rather than in the muscle, so the flesh is among the leanest of commercial fish at well under 1 percent fat. The fillet tapers hard: the loin, the thick section just behind the head and above the ribcage, runs 1.5 to 2 inches on a decent fish, and the tail thins to half an inch or less. Those are two different ingredients and they should never go in the pan at the same time. Pacific cod is a slightly smaller, firmer, higher-yield relative of Atlantic cod and cooks essentially the same way.

Why it cooks the way it does

Take a muscle that's 81 percent water, remove the fat, and give it thin connective seams, and you get a fish with no buffer of any kind. Fish collagen gelatinizes far lower than mammal collagen — cod's myocommata start dissolving somewhere around 120-130°F, so the structure holding the flakes together is failing at the same moment the fibers are contracting and pushing out water. That's the specific reason cod goes from set to disintegrating over about ten degrees. There's a second clock running too: cod flesh is high in trimethylamine oxide, which bacteria reduce to trimethylamine after harvest, and TMA is the ammonia-fishy smell — genuinely fresh cod smells like almost nothing. Salt fixes both problems by a different route: a 5 percent brine for 20-30 minutes, or a full salt cure for days, draws water out and firms the protein matrix so the fillet holds together under heat instead of falling into pieces.

How to cook it

Brine and roast at 400°F
Why it works

Dry heat from all sides on a thick loin gives an even gradient, and the brine has already firmed the proteins so the flakes hold their shape as the seams soften.

How

5 percent salt brine, 20-30 minutes, then dry the surface thoroughly. Oil it, 400°F, 10-12 minutes for a 1.5-inch loin. Pull at 130°F, when the flakes shift under gentle pressure but the center is still slightly glossy.

Pan-sear a brined loin
Why it works

A hard crust on one face gives the fillet a rigid side to turn on, which is the difference between a piece of cod and a pan of cod pieces.

How

Bone-dry surface, oil to shimmering in a heavy pan over medium-high, lay the fish down presentation side first and do not move it for 3-4 minutes. One flip with a fish spatula, 2-3 more minutes, basting with butter. Pull at 130°F.

Low poach in oil or court bouillon
Why it works

At 150-160°F the surrounding liquid is only twenty degrees above the target, so there's no runaway edge and the fish physically can't overshoot much even if you're distracted.

How

Submerge the salted loin in olive oil or a seasoned broth held at 150-160°F — small movement, no bubbles. 12-18 minutes for 1.5 inches. Done when a paring knife slides in with no resistance.

Batter and fry at 360°F
Why it works

The batter is a rigid shell that traps steam, so the cod cooks in its own moisture inside a container that keeps every flake in place.

How

Dry the fish, dust with flour, dip in a cold, thin beer batter. Oil at 350-360°F, 4-6 minutes for a 1-inch piece, two at a time so the temperature doesn't drop below 325°F. The batter should be set and pale gold, not dark brown — dark means the oil was too hot and the fish inside is overcooked.

What ruins it

Cooking until it flakes easily with a fork

That test is a trap on cod specifically. A fork separates cod flakes freely only after the connective seams have fully gelatinized, which happens right alongside fiber contraction — so the moment the test reads positive you're at 145-150°F and the fillet has already dumped its water. Cod is 81 percent water and under 1 percent fat, so there's no marbling and no gelatin left in reserve to occupy the space that water left; the flakes go from dense and translucent to dry, stringy, and cottony, and they crumble into shreds you can't lift onto a plate in one piece. There's no recovery, because you'd be trying to put water back inside cells that have already contracted and pushed it out. Pull at 130°F, when the flakes shift against each other under light pressure but still want to hold together.

Buying it

Ask for loin, not fillet — the thick front section, an even 1.5 to 2 inches, cut into portions rather than a whole tapering side. If you're handed a tail piece, cook it separately or fold it under itself so it's uniform thickness. Fresh cod should smell like clean water and nothing else; any ammonia or 'fishy' note is trimethylamine, which means bacteria have been working on it. Press it: the flesh should be firm and resilient, and the flakes should be tight together. Gaping — visible splits between the muscle blocks — means the seams have already started dissolving from age or a bad freeze-thaw, and that fillet cannot be saved. Most Pacific cod is headed, gutted, and frozen on the boat, and a single-frozen 'frozen at sea' fillet is generally in better shape than 'fresh' cod that's been thawed at the counter for two days; twice-frozen product goes watery and leaks in the pan. For salt cod, look for firm, cream-white boneless sides, not yellowed or floppy ones.

Doneness: Pull at 130°F, serve at 130-135°F. The cue is flakes that shift under gentle pressure with the center still slightly translucent and glossy — if they separate on their own you've gone too far. FDA's 145°F guideline is a safety floor, not a texture target, and on a fish this lean the fifteen degrees between them is the whole difference. Salt cod and fried cod are judged by texture, not a probe.

Straight answers

What's the difference between cod and haddock?

They're both gadoids and they cook nearly identically, but haddock has finer, softer flakes and a slightly sweeter flavor, while cod has larger flakes and a firmer bite. Cod loin is thicker, so it holds together better in a pan or on a sheet pan; haddock is the traditional choice for smoking (finnan haddie) and holds up well in batter. You can swap them one for one — just check thickness, since haddock fillets tend to run thinner and will finish a couple minutes earlier.

Why does my cod fall apart in the pan?

Three usual causes. It's overcooked — the connective seams gelatinize by about 130-135°F and past that there's nothing holding the flakes to each other. It's wet — a surface with water on it steams instead of searing, so no crust forms and there's no rigid face to turn it on. Or it's been frozen and thawed more than once, which ruptures cells and destroys structure before you ever heat it. A 20-30 minute soak in 5 percent salt brine, then a thoroughly dried surface, then a hot pan you don't touch for four minutes fixes the first two.

Is frozen cod as good as fresh?

Usually better, if it's frozen-at-sea and only frozen once. Cod deteriorates fast — the trimethylamine that produces that ammonia smell builds up within days — so a fillet frozen solid on the boat within hours of harvest beats one that spent five days in transit and two more in a display case. What you want to avoid is twice-frozen fish, which loses water and turns spongy. Thaw it overnight in the fridge, never in water, and pat it bone dry before cooking.

What internal temperature should cod be cooked to?

Pull at 130°F for the best texture, which puts it at 130-135°F on the plate. At that point the flakes shift under gentle pressure but still cling together, and the center looks slightly translucent and glossy. The commonly cited 145°F is a food-safety guideline, and on a fish with under 1 percent fat those extra fifteen degrees are the difference between dense and cottony. If you're cooking for someone who needs the safety margin, poach it in oil at 150-160°F so the fat compensates for the water it loses.
