The Cut Guide

Ground Pork

Also sold as: pork mince · minced pork · fresh ground pork · bulk sausage grind

Ground pork is almost always shoulder trim, which means it comes with 20–30% fat already in it and a lot of chopped collagen. That fat is softer than beef fat and melts at a lower temperature, which is both the reason ground pork stays moist in a dumpling filling and the reason it turns greasy the moment it gets warm before cooking. Temperature control during grinding and mixing matters more here than with any other grind.

This is a cut guide, not a recipe — what this cut is, why it behaves the way it does, and which of our 29 dishes use it. Recipes stay ad-free; guides like this one carry a single ad.

What it is

The shoulder — Boston butt and picnic — is what almost all fresh ground pork is made from, plus jowl and back fat blended in at some shops to hit a ratio. A pig has no collarbone; the front legs are slung to the body by muscle alone, so the shoulder carries the front third of the animal's weight and works constantly at low intensity. That builds a dense collagen network, fat seams between a dozen small muscles running in different directions, and heavy intramuscular fat inside the muscles themselves. The grinder erases all of that architecture, which is exactly the point: the same tissue that needs eight hours as a roast needs eight minutes as a grind. Ground pork sold as 'lean' has usually been cut with loin or leg trim, and it behaves like a different product.

Why it cooks the way it does

Pork fat is high in oleic acid, so it's soft at room temperature and starts melting in the 85–95°F range — well below beef tallow, which holds solid past 110°F. That low melting point is why ground pork stays moist in a filling and why warm pork fat smears rather than chops. Smearing is the central failure in every ground pork preparation: once fat has been pushed into a paste and pressed onto the surface of the protein particles, it no longer sits in discrete pockets, and on cooking it runs straight out of the product. The fix is thermal — meat and grinder parts at 32–34°F, or the meat partially frozen with firm edges. On the other side, salt at 1.5–2% of the meat's weight dissolves myosin into a tacky sol that traps both the fat particles and added water; that protein network is the entire structure of a sausage or a meatball. Collagen from the shoulder is chopped fine enough to gelatinize during a short cook, so ground pork holds together and gives at the same time.

How to cook it

Sausage and patties
Why it works

Salt-extracted myosin plus cold fat particles builds a matrix that traps rendered fat instead of shedding it, so a 70/30 patty reads as moist rather than greasy.

How

Meat at 32–34°F. Salt at 1.5–1.8% by weight, seasoning, then 2–3 oz ice water per lb. Mix by hand or paddle 60–90 seconds until the mass is tacky and pulls in one piece. Rest overnight in the fridge for the salt to work through. Cook patties 3–4 minutes a side to 160°F.

Dumpling, wonton, and meatball filling
Why it works

Pork fat that melts near 95°F liquefies inside the wrapper during steaming and, held by the myosin network, stays there as the soup in a soup dumpling instead of running off.

How

Coarse grind or hand-chopped, 25–30% fat. Salt, then beat in stock or water in stages — 1/4 to 1/3 cup per lb — stirring in one direction until fully absorbed and the mix is glossy. Keep everything cold. Steam 8–10 minutes for dumplings.

Crumbled hard in a wok or skillet
Why it works

Fine crumbles in a very hot pan brown and partly dry out, which concentrates them into a seasoning rather than a protein — this is what mapo tofu, dan dan noodles, and larb are built on.

How

Screaming hot pan, thin layer, don't stir for the first 2 minutes. Fry until the crumbles darken and some edges go crisp, 5–8 minutes total. The rendered fat is the cooking medium for the aromatics that follow — don't pour it off.

Blended into beef for meatballs and meatloaf
Why it works

Pork fat renders at a lower temperature than beef fat and keeps softening the matrix while beef proteins are contracting, so a mixed ball sets softer than an all-beef one at the same internal temp.

How

Common ratios are half pork and half beef, or a third each pork, beef, and veal. Salt at 1%, panade of bread and milk, bake or simmer to 160°F.

What ruins it

Letting the meat and fat warm up before or during mixing

Above roughly 40°F pork fat softens enough to shear into a paste instead of chopping into particles. Once it has smeared it coats the protein surfaces and can't be redispersed — you can't unbreak it by chilling afterward, because the particle structure is gone. The result cooks two ways at once: fat pours out of the product into the pan while the meat inside, no longer holding any of it, comes out dry. A sausage that renders into a puddle and still tastes dry is always a smeared batch, not a seasoning problem. Chill the meat to 32–34°F, freeze the grinder auger and plate, and work in small batches.

Buying it

Ask for shoulder grind and ask for the ratio; a good counter runs ground pork somewhere between 70/30 and 80/20 and will tell you which. Avoid anything labeled lean or extra-lean unless you're adding fat back, because below about 15% fat ground pork goes chalky. A single pass through a 3/16 or 1/4 inch plate is what you want — you should see distinct white fat flecks against pink, not a homogeneous pale paste. Check the label for 'enhanced' or a solution percentage: injected pork carries added water and phosphate, which will not sear and will weep in a filling. 'Ground pork' is plain meat; 'pork sausage' is already salted and seasoned, so don't season it again to a recipe's spec. If you grind your own, use a Boston butt with the fat cap on and it lands near 75/25 without any blending.

Doneness: 160°F is the USDA target for ground pork, same grinding logic as ground beef. Whole-muscle pork drops to 145°F with a rest, but a grind doesn't get that. Texturally, 160°F is where the myosin network is fully set and a sausage springs back to a press; past 170°F the fat has largely rendered out and the texture goes granular.

Straight answers

Can I substitute ground pork for ground beef?

In sauces, meatballs, chili, and fillings, yes — one for one. The differences are fat behavior and flavor: pork fat melts lower so the dish reads softer and richer, and pork brings less of the iron-and-mineral flavor that beef has. In a burger it's a real substitution rather than a swap, because a pork patty needs to be cooked to 160°F where a beef patty doesn't, and the texture at that temperature is firmer. Half pork and half beef is the standard compromise for meatballs and meatloaf.

Why is my homemade sausage dry and crumbly?

Usually fat content or fat smearing. Below about 20% fat there isn't enough to fill the space the water leaves at 160°F. If the ratio is right, the fat smeared: the meat got above 40°F somewhere in the grind or the mix, the fat sheared into a paste, and it rendered straight out during cooking. The third cause is not mixing long enough — without 60–90 seconds of hard mixing after salting, myosin never dissolves and there's no protein network holding anything, so the sausage falls apart in the pan.

Do I have to cook ground pork to 160°F?

Yes, for the same reason as ground beef — grinding distributes surface bacteria through the whole batch, so there's no sterile interior. The 145°F figure people remember applies to intact cuts like chops and roasts, with a 3-minute rest. Trichinella, the historical reason for cooking pork hard, dies at 137°F and is essentially absent from commercial US pork; the 160°F number is about Salmonella and the grind.

What's the difference between ground pork and ground pork sausage?

Ground pork is unseasoned fresh meat. Pork sausage is the same grind with salt at roughly 1.5–2%, usually sage, black pepper, and sometimes sugar or cure, and it has often been mixed hard enough to develop a protein bind. That means sausage is already salted — if a recipe calls for ground pork plus salt and you use bulk sausage, you'll overshoot. It also means sausage has a springier texture, which is wrong for dumpling fillings where you want a loose crumb.
