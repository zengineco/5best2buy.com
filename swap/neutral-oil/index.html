What to use instead of neutral oil

It depends entirely on what it's doing in the dish. Here's the job, what else does it, and what to skip.

Advertisement

Deep-frying medium

It has to sit at 175-190C without smoking, taste of nothing, and survive several batches without breaking down into something bitter.

Peanut oilRan out
1:1
Nothing you will taste, and it takes more abuse before it degrades. Off the table for anyone with a peanut allergy.
Where to buy it →
Refined sunflower or safflower oilNut-free
1:1
No difference in the finished food. It breaks down a batch or two sooner on repeat frying.
Where to buy it →
Beef tallow or lardRan out
1:1 by volume
Fries harder and crisper with a savoury edge, and chips reheat better. It congeals on the plate as the food cools.
Where to buy it →
Refined coconut oilRan out
1:1
Neutral in the refined form, but it sets solid on the fried coating within minutes and the crust turns waxy.
Where to buy it →
What doesn't work: Extra virgin olive oil in a deep fryer. Its aroma compounds break down and go bitter well before the oil smokes, so the whole batch tastes scorched, and a full pot of it costs more than the food.

Searing and stir-frying in a hot pan

A thin film that carries heat into the surface of the food and stops it sticking, without smoking the kitchen out at wok temperature.

Avocado oilRan out
1:1
Nothing you will taste, and the highest smoke point of the common bottles.
Where to buy it →
Peanut oilRan out
1:1
Faintly nutty at high heat, which is what a wok dish wants anyway.
Where to buy it →
GheeRan out
1:1
Browns the food faster and tastes buttery. It burns sooner than its smoke point suggests if the pan sits empty.
Where to buy it →
Beef tallow or duck fatRan out
1:1
Better crust on meat and potatoes and a savoury flavour. Congeals on the plate.
Where to buy it →
What doesn't work: Toasted sesame oil as the cooking fat. It smokes at low heat and turns acrid. Use it off the heat by the teaspoon at the end.

Fat in a batter or dough where you want no flavour

It coats flour and shortens the crumb, and being liquid at room temperature it keeps a cake soft even straight out of the fridge.

Melted butterRan out
1 1/4 cups melted butter per 1 cup oil
Browns more and tastes of butter. The cake firms up hard in the fridge where an oil cake stays soft.
Where to buy it →
Light olive oilRan out
1:1
Barely registers. Extra virgin in the same place tastes grassy and peppery in a plain vanilla cake.
Where to buy it →
Melted coconut oilRan out
1:1
Cake sets firmer and reads faintly of coconut. It seizes into lumps if the eggs and milk are cold, so bring everything to room temperature.
Where to buy it →
Unsweetened applesauce, for half the oil onlyRan out
Replace half the oil 1:1 by volume
Springier and slightly gummy, and it stales a day sooner. Taking out all the oil gives you a rubbery cake.
What doesn't work: Cold butter cut into the flour where the recipe says oil. Solid fat builds flakes and layers, liquid fat builds a fine even crumb, so the muffin comes out short and crumbly instead of moist.
